Updated April 27, 2026

TSA Updated Its Medical Marijuana Guidance

Guidance published by the Transportation Security Administration

TSA's official “What Can I Bring?” page now lists medical marijuana as permitted in both bag types — each with the same qualifier attached.

Carry-on bags Yes (Special Instructions)
Checked bags Yes (Special Instructions)
TSA, in its own words

“TSA's screening procedures are focused on security and are designed to detect potential threats to aviation and passengers. Accordingly, TSA security officers do not search for illegal drugs, but if any illegal substance or evidence of criminal activity is discovered during security screening, TSA will refer the matter to a law enforcement officer.”

— Transportation Security Administration, “Medical Marijuana”

That sounds like a major change, and in one sense it is: an agency page that used to read as a flat prohibition now reads as conditional permission. But it should not be read as blanket permission to bring any type of cannabis onto an airplane, and the two words doing the heavy lifting are “medical” and “Special Instructions.”

Read the quote again and note what it does not say. It does not say cannabis is cleared for travel — it says officers aren't looking for it, and that they will hand the matter over if they find it. The final call on whether any item passes the checkpoint still rests with the TSA officer on duty.

Bottom line: permitted is not the same as guaranteed. The officer at the checkpoint still decides, and a referral to law enforcement is still on the table.

Part One

What the TSA Update Actually Means

Four questions that decide whether the new wording applies to you at all — and what happens if it doesn't.

Question 01

Does This Mean You Can Fly With Weed?

Short answer: not necessarily The page says “medical marijuana”

The updated TSA page specifically refers to medical marijuana — not recreational cannabis purchased for adult use. That distinction is the whole ballgame, and it is where most of the coverage went wrong.

The new wording does not mean:

  • All cannabis products are federally legal
  • A California dispensary purchase can be carried into every state
  • A medical marijuana card guarantees passage through security
  • An airline must allow cannabis aboard its aircraft
  • Cannabis can be taken across an international border
  • TSA will never refer a traveler to airport police

VICE described the change as a sign that airport cannabis policies are beginning to acknowledge what is already happening among travelers. The same piece also warned that the update is not a federal green light to pack recreational flower for a trip: recreational cannabis remains federally restricted, state laws vary, and international travel presents a completely different level of risk.

Question 02

Why Did TSA Change Its Medical Marijuana Page?

Rule published April 28, 2026 Two narrow Schedule III categories

The timing was not a coincidence. The TSA update happened immediately before an important change in federal marijuana policy. A federal rule published on April 28, 2026 placed two limited categories of marijuana into Schedule III:

  • FDA-approved drug products containing marijuana
  • Marijuana subject to a qualifying state medical marijuana license

Marijuana that does not fit within one of those two categories can remain classified as a Schedule I controlled substance. The separate, broader effort to reschedule marijuana generally was still working its way through the federal rulemaking and hearing process.

This distinction is the reason the TSA page reads the way it does. It should not be interpreted as the federal legalization of recreational cannabis, or as permission to transport unlicensed cannabis products.

Question 03

Medical Marijuana Versus Recreational Cannabis

A card is evidence, not a conversion Original packaging matters

California allows both medical and adult-use cannabis. The updated TSA page identifies only medical marijuana.

A California medical recommendation or patient card may help establish that a traveler is a medical cannabis patient. It does not automatically convert recreational cannabis, homemade products, or cannabis obtained from an unlicensed source into federally recognized medical marijuana. Those are two different things, and only one of them is what the TSA page is talking about.

When carrying a qualifying medical product, consider keeping:

  • The product in its original dispensary packaging
  • The original product label still attached
  • A purchase receipt from the licensed medical retailer
  • A current medical marijuana card or physician documentation
  • Only a reasonable personal-use quantity

None of this guarantees a product will be allowed through security. What it does is make the product's origin and intended medical use clear at a glance, which is a materially better position than a loose jar with no label.

Question 04

What Happens If TSA Finds Cannabis?

Officers screen for aviation threats Local police decide the outcome

TSA officers are primarily looking for weapons, explosives and other threats to aviation. If cannabis is noticed while a bag is being screened, TSA can contact the law enforcement agency responsible for that airport. The responding officers then determine what happens next, based on the product, the quantity, the documentation and the applicable laws.

Possible outcomes can depend on:

  • Whether the product qualifies as medical marijuana
  • Whether the traveler has valid medical documentation
  • Whether the product came from a licensed medical dispensary
  • The amount being carried
  • The laws in the departure and destination states
  • The airport's local enforcement policy
  • The airline's individual rules

Note what the TSA page does not provide: a universal passenger quantity limit, a documentation checklist, or any guarantee that a medical marijuana card will prevent a law enforcement referral. The absence of those specifics is exactly why “Special Instructions” is not the same as “allowed.”

Part Two

The Rules at LAX and in the Air

Seven practical questions covering the airport itself, your device batteries, the cabin, your airline's own policy, and wherever you're landing.

Question 05

What Is the Marijuana Policy at LAX?

Los Angeles International Airport LAX Airport Police Division

LAX publishes its own marijuana policy. Under California law, adults who are at least 21 years old may possess up to:

  • 28.5 grams of cannabis flower
  • 8 grams of concentrated cannabis

The Los Angeles Airport Police Division states that its officers do not have jurisdiction to arrest someone who is complying with California possession laws. That is a meaningful protection on the public side of the airport.

LAX also warns travelers that TSA screening checkpoints are under federal jurisdiction, and that marijuana laws change from state to state — so travelers must review the laws at their destination before leaving California.

In plain terms: legally possessing cannabis in the terminal's public areas does not automatically mean TSA, your airline, or the authorities at your destination will allow it. Those are four separate gates, and the airport only controls one of them.

Official LAX policy
Question 06

Can You Pack a Cannabis Vape in Checked Luggage?

No — carry-on only FAA lithium battery rule

No. Vape devices and spare lithium batteries must not be placed in checked luggage. This is a battery rule, not a cannabis rule, and it applies to the hardware regardless of what's in it.

The Federal Aviation Administration requires electronic smoking devices — including vape pens and vaporizers — to be carried on the passenger or inside carry-on baggage. Travelers must also take effective steps to prevent the device from accidentally turning on.

  • No vape devices or spare lithium batteries in checked bags
  • No charging the device aboard the aircraft
  • No unprotected spare batteries — they must be guarded against short circuits

These rules sit entirely separately from cannabis law. A traveler could be carrying a perfectly permitted product and still have packed the hardware incorrectly — which is its own problem at the checkpoint.

FAA PackSafe guidance
Question 07

Can You Use Cannabis at LAX or During a Flight?

No smoking or vaping, anywhere Time your edibles carefully

No. California possession laws do not authorize smoking or vaping cannabis inside airport terminals, aboard an aircraft, or in any other location where smoking is prohibited.

Never smoke, vape or openly consume cannabis while waiting at the terminal or on the plane. Being allowed to possess a product is not the same as being allowed to use it wherever you choose — and an aircraft lavatory is one of the worst places anyone has ever tried it.

Edibles deserve their own warning. They can take considerably longer to produce noticeable effects, and they may feel more intense or last longer than expected. Avoid taking an unfamiliar product immediately before entering a crowded airport or boarding a flight — onset arriving somewhere over Nevada, on a full plane, is not the experience anyone is after.

Question 08

Should Cannabis Be Stored in a Smell-Proof Bag?

Courtesy and containment Not a concealment tool

A properly sealed smell-proof container is a considerate and practical way to store a legal cannabis product. Flower can produce a strong odor that spreads through luggage, rideshares and public spaces, and other travelers should not have to smell someone else's cannabis for the length of an airport and a flight.

VICE's national travel guide recommends modern smell-proof bags and cases for organizing legal hemp products, permitted medical cannabis and related accessories, highlighting compact pouches, locking storage cases and larger smell-resistant travel bags.

A smell-proof bag can help:

  • Control strong cannabis odors
  • Keep flower and pre-rolls from spilling
  • Protect glass jars and vape cartridges
  • Keep cannabis products separate from clothing
  • Prevent children or other unauthorized people from accessing the product, when a locking case is used

To be direct about it: smell-proof storage does not make an illegal product legal. Use it for cleanliness, organization and consideration for other travelers — not to hide cannabis from TSA or law enforcement. And keep the original dispensary packaging inside the sealed bag rather than throwing it away; the label is the thing that establishes where the product came from.

Question 09

Does Your Airline Allow Medical Marijuana?

Airline rules are separate Check every connecting carrier

TSA rules and airline rules are two different things. An airline can establish its own restrictions on marijuana, vaping devices and controlled substances, and a product appearing as “Yes” on TSA's website does not require an airline to accept it.

Before traveling, review the prohibited-item policy published by the airline actually operating your flight. This matters most on itineraries with connecting flights operated by different carriers — the policy that applies can change partway through the trip, on the same ticket.

Question 10

Can You Fly Internationally With Medical Marijuana?

The safest answer is no Customs, import and export law

No. Traveling internationally introduces customs, import and export laws on top of TSA screening. A product that qualifies as medical marijuana in California may be flatly prohibited on entry to another country, and the penalties in some jurisdictions are severe.

This includes destinations where cannabis is legal under that country's own domestic laws. Domestic legalization does not authorize a traveler to carry cannabis across an international border. The 2026 federal medical marijuana rule also maintains import and export permit requirements for covered marijuana products.

Never rely on the TSA medical marijuana page as permission to bring cannabis into another country. It has no bearing on what another nation's customs authority will do.

Federal Register rule
Question 11

Check the Laws at Your Destination

Legal in LA, illegal on landing Layovers count too

Possession that is perfectly legal in Los Angeles can become illegal the moment you land. Some states allow recreational cannabis. Others allow medical use only, require registration in their own medical program, or do not recognize medical cards issued by another state. Certain states still impose serious penalties for possession.

Before you fly, confirm:

  • Whether cannabis is legal at the destination
  • Whether that state recognizes out-of-state medical patients
  • The state's possession limits
  • Whether the specific product type is permitted
  • Whether a connecting airport sits in a state with different laws

Do not assume a nonstop flight and a connecting flight carry the same level of risk. A layover puts you on the ground, with your bags, inside a jurisdiction you never intended to visit.

The Three Things That Actually Decide It

If you read nothing else on this page, read these — especially if you're flying out of LAX rather than into it.

The Checkpoint Is Federal Ground

California's possession limits govern the public side of the airport, and LAX Airport Police state they have no jurisdiction to arrest a compliant adult 21+. Step into the TSA screening line and you have stepped into federal jurisdiction, where the officer decides what passes and a referral to law enforcement remains possible.

Vape Hardware Has Its Own Rules

Separate from cannabis law entirely: the FAA requires vape pens, vaporizers and spare lithium batteries to travel in the cabin, never in checked bags. Prevent accidental activation, protect spare batteries against short circuits, and do not charge the device in flight.

Your Destination Overrules Everything

Clearing LAX is not the finish line. The laws that matter most are the ones where you land — plus any state you connect through. Out-of-state medical cards are frequently not recognized, possession limits differ, and product types allowed in California may not be allowed elsewhere.

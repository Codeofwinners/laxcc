# California Just Changed the Rules on Cannabis in Your Car — Here's What Every Driver Needs to Know

**Published:** January 30, 2026  
**Category:** Cannabis Law & Education  
**Author:** LAX Cannabis Club

---

On January 30, 2026, the California Supreme Court handed down one of the most significant cannabis-related legal decisions since voters passed Prop 64 back in 2016. If you drive in California and you consume cannabis — or even if you've ever had a crumb of flower land on your floorboard — this ruling directly affects your rights. We're breaking it all down so you know exactly where you stand.

## The Case That Changed Everything

It started with a routine traffic stop in Sacramento on November 5, 2021. A man named Davonyae Sellers was riding as a passenger in the front seat of a car that was pulled over for failing to stop behind a limit line. Two Sacramento Police Department officers — Officer Conner Mills and Officer Mark Thrall — looked into the back of the vehicle and spotted two things: a joint-rolling tray on the back seat, and a few scattered marijuana crumbs on the rear floorboard.

That's it. No one was driving erratically. The driver had a clean record, a valid license, and no outstanding warrants. The total amount of cannabis found? **0.36 grams** — the approximate weight of a single dollar bill, or roughly the contents of one average joint, scattered loosely across the floor.

Officers used the presence of those crumbs and the rolling tray to claim "probable cause" under California's open container law, which gave them justification to conduct a full search of the vehicle. During that search, an unregistered handgun was found in the car. Sellers was subsequently charged with unlawful possession of a firearm under Penal Code §29820.

What followed was four and a half years of litigation over whether floor crumbs legally constitute an "open container" of cannabis.

## Four Years of Courts Getting It Wrong

Sellers challenged the search from the start, arguing that the officers had no legitimate probable cause to search the vehicle. His motion to suppress was denied by a magistrate judge. Sacramento Superior Court Judge Deborah D. Lobre upheld that denial in November 2023. Then on August 22, 2024, the Third District Court of Appeal denied his petition as well, with Justice Peter A. Krause and Presiding Justice Laurie M. Earl ruling that the crumbs were enough to establish probable cause.

The one voice of reason at that stage was Court of Appeal Justice Elana Duarte, who dissented and called it what it was: a targeted traffic stop of a vehicle carrying Black and Hispanic occupants. She noted it would be absurd to criminalize a few scattered marijuana particles while legalizing a sealed baggie sitting in the front seat.

The case eventually made its way to the California Supreme Court, where the justices took a very different view.

## The Supreme Court's Unanimous Decision

On January 29 to 30, 2026, the California Supreme Court issued a unanimous ruling in Sellers' favor, authored by Associate Justice Goodwin Liu. The court's holding is now the binding legal standard across the entire state:

> **"We hold that at a minimum, to constitute a violation of the open container law, marijuana in a vehicle must be of a usable quantity, in imminently usable condition, and readily accessible to an occupant."**

In plain English: if you can't actually consume it in the moment, it does not trigger the open container law.

The court drew a clear and practical distinction between cannabis and alcohol. Grabbing an open beer from the back seat and drinking it while driving is easy. Collecting loose flower crumbs off a floorboard, packing a pipe, or rolling a joint while operating a vehicle is not. The risk to public safety — which is what the open container law was designed to address — simply isn't present when the substance can't be readily consumed.

Justice Liu also addressed a potential loophole in the opposite direction. He gave the example of marijuana gummies dumped loose into a center console versus the same gummies in an open bag. Both scenarios would violate the law, because in both cases the gummies are accessible and ready to consume. The law, he wrote, is about **accessibility and usability** — not about whether the cannabis happens to be inside a physical container.

The court also ruled that the officers had no probable cause to search the car at all. The driver's nervousness and the presence of a rolling tray were not sufficient legal justification. The search was unlawful from the start.

## What This Means for You Practically

The ruling establishes a three-part test that cannabis must meet before it can be considered an "open container" violation in California:

**1. Usable quantity.** A few crumbs or trace amounts of residue do not qualify. The cannabis must be present in an amount that could actually be used.

**2. Imminently usable condition.** Loose flower scattered on a floorboard is not in a usable condition. A pre-rolled joint that's ready to smoke is. Packed bowl, ready to light — that counts. Flower that needs to be collected, ground, and then rolled or packed does not.

**3. Readily accessible to an occupant.** Cannabis in the trunk, in a sealed container, or in the back of the vehicle while all occupants are in the front does not meet this standard. The court specifically noted that no officer even suggested Sellers could have realistically collected those backseat crumbs from his front-seat position for immediate consumption.

## The Bigger Picture

This ruling matters beyond the specifics of one traffic stop. For years, vague interpretations of the open container law gave law enforcement a low-bar pretext for vehicle searches in cannabis-legal California. A rolling tray and a few crumbs — neither of which represent any real threat of impaired driving — were enough to justify a full search of someone's car under prior court interpretations.

The Supreme Court has now closed that door. As Justice Liu wrote, the entire purpose of the open container law is to reduce impaired driving by limiting ready access to intoxicating substances. Marijuana that cannot realistically be consumed while driving poses no such risk, and pretending otherwise was never consistent with the spirit of Prop 64.

This is a win for civil liberties, for equity, and for the millions of Californians who legally consume cannabis and deserve to be treated accordingly.
